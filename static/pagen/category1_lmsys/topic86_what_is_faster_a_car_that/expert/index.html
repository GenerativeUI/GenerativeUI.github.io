The Science Behind Speed Perception

Our brains perceive speed relative to effort, environment, and motion cues. In a car, smooth suspension and protection make 20 km/h feel slow, while on a bike, exposure to wind and effort make it feel fast.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about speed, cycling, and urban transportation

Is 20 km/h fast for a bicycle?

20 km/h is considered a moderate to good cycling speed for most people. Recreational cyclists typically average 15-20 km/h, while commuters often maintain 18-25 km/h. Professional cyclists can exceed 40 km/h on flat terrain.

Why does the average car speed in cities seem so low?

Urban car speeds are limited by traffic lights, congestion, pedestrian crossings, and speed limits. Even on roads with 50 km/h limits, actual average speeds often drop to 20-30 km/h due to frequent stops and traffic flow interruptions.

What's the typical cycling commute distance?

Most cycling commutes range from 3-15 km, with 5-10 km being the sweet spot for many urban commuters. At 20 km/h, a 10 km commute takes about 30 minutes, making it competitive with car travel in congested cities.

Which is more energy efficient at 20 km/h?

Bicycles are dramatically more energy efficient. A cyclist uses about 0.1 kWh per 20 km, while a car uses 1-2 kWh for the same distance. The bicycle is 10-20 times more efficient, converting human energy directly into motion with minimal waste.

How do weather conditions affect both vehicles?

Weather significantly impacts cycling comfort and safety, while cars are largely weather-independent. Rain, wind, and extreme temperatures can make cycling challenging or dangerous, whereas cars provide protection and climate control regardless of conditions.

What about parking and accessibility?

Bicycles excel in urban parking - they can be secured almost anywhere and often closer to destinations. Cars require dedicated parking spaces and may face restrictions in city centers. Bikes also access bike lanes, paths, and areas where cars cannot go.
